## Light your eyes can&rsquo;t see

Every print in this catalogue begins as something invisible. Light at
wavelengths the human eye can&rsquo;t register &mdash; near-infrared,
shortwave-infrared, thermal &mdash; bouncing off forests, oceans, sand,
ice. Different materials emit and reflect differently across the
spectrum. Healthy vegetation lights up in near-infrared. Iron-rich rock
hums in shortwave-infrared. Shallow water collapses to a deeper blue
than your eyes ever see. The information is real and physical; the only
problem is your retinas don&rsquo;t catch most of it.

The work that follows is, in essence, a translation. Light captured in
wavelengths we can&rsquo;t see &mdash; mapped onto the visible spectrum
so we can.

## The instruments

The capture is done by satellites that have spent decades learning to
look. **Landsat** is a joint program of NASA and the U.S. Geological
Survey, the longest-running Earth observation record we have &mdash;
unbroken since 1972. Landsat 8 and 9 carry the **Operational Land
Imager** (OLI), reading nine spectral bands from deep blue out to
shortwave-infrared. **Sentinel-2** is the European Space Agency&rsquo;s
contribution under the Copernicus programme, covering Earth every five
days at 10 m resolution. **ASTER**, riding on NASA&rsquo;s Terra
satellite, adds a thermal eye that can resolve a 1&deg;C temperature
difference on the ground.

These instruments cost hundreds of millions of dollars to design,
launch, and operate. Every one of them is the product of a decade or
more of optical engineering, mission planning, and international
diplomacy. They&rsquo;re the most carefully made image-makers in human
history. The catalogue rests on their work.

## The scientists

A raw satellite scene is just numbers &mdash; multispectral arrays,
calibration coefficients, ground-control points. To turn that into
something you can look at, **remote-sensing scientists at the USGS
Earth Resources Observation and Science (EROS) Center** in South Dakota
do the slow, careful work of choosing band combinations, stretching
histograms, georeferencing, mosaicking, and composing. The full-colour
plates that show up in the **Earth as Art** series are theirs &mdash; a
public-domain collection assembled across two decades by some of the
best image scientists working today. When a scene in this catalogue
reads as art, it&rsquo;s because EROS scientists earned the read.

The studio doesn&rsquo;t pretend that the imagery is original. It
isn&rsquo;t. The composites are theirs. What the studio does is
*choose*.

## The studio

What Orbital Artifacts contributes is editorial: which images out of
hundreds, sequenced in what order, framed with what coordinates, on
what paper. Curation is a real practice with a long history &mdash; the
job of looking at a vast archive and saying *this one earns a print, and
this one comes after it.* It&rsquo;s the same job a record-label A&amp;R
does, or a museum&rsquo;s acquisitions committee, or the editor of a
photography monograph.

Every print carries its own catalogue number, latitude, longitude, and
acquisition date, set in a restrained monospaced font on the mat. A
buyer who wants to recognise the place can drop a pin on a map and
verify it. A buyer who just wants the colour can ignore the metadata
entirely. The frame rewards both.

## The print

Files print on **archival matte cotton paper, 320 gsm**, with pigment
inks rated for over a century of light resistance. Each print is signed
and numbered against a small edition &mdash; the catalogue runs editions
between 8 and 32 prints depending on the scene, and *we don&rsquo;t
restock*. Once a piece is sold out, the only way to own it is on a
secondary market.

Fulfilment is print-on-demand through Printify or Gelato. Both run a
careful colour pipeline; we&rsquo;ve printed test sets across both and
the studio chooses the provider per scene based on which renders the
specific palette best. The print ships from whichever facility is
nearest the buyer, which keeps shipping fast and the carbon footprint
honest.

## What the price reflects

A satellite. An imaging sensor in low Earth orbit, calibrated against
the sun. The team at the USGS EROS Center who turned that calibration
into a colour plate. Two decades of public-domain Earth observation,
released as a gift to anyone who wants to use it. The studio&rsquo;s
selection, sequencing, and framing. Archival paper. Pigment ink. A
small edition with a hard ceiling.

Compare that chain to a stock-photo poster, which has none of it.

## Originally-processed work

Originally-processed scenes &mdash; built in the studio from raw Landsat
or Sentinel data rather than curated from the EaA archive &mdash; are
planned but not yet on this catalogue. When they ship, they&rsquo;ll
carry the studio&rsquo;s own band choices and a separate processing
note. Until then, every print here is a **curated edit** of work that
already belongs to the public, framed by the studio for the wall.
